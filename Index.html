Introduction

Why I lift

My fitness journey began long before I thought about mental health or personal philosophy. I started with a mindset rooted in self-hatred, thinking that building muscle would somehow fix those feelings.

The real benefit the gym has brought me goes beyond physical change. It has transformed the way I think and perceive the world. The constant progress, both physical and mental, and the peace of mind it gives me have been the real game changers.

As I continued, I realized that letting go of shame and the fear of what others think opens up a whole new world. I started to find joy in even the smallest things. Something as simple as a smile can brighten someone’s day in ways you might never imagine. You never know what someone is going through, so don’t be too quick to judge.

None of this happens overnight. It takes months, even years, of learning, growing and refining who you really are. The journey never ends, and the more you commit to it, the more you evolve.

True strength lies not just in physical progress, but in how we carry ourselves and treat others along the way.

Philosophy

Stoicism

I first got into philosophy because of Berserk. I read it during a difficult period in my life, and the story combined with my own experiences triggered a desire to grow.

For me, Stoicism is the art of letting go of everything beyond your control. Some people link it with depression or a somber outlook, but that is only one way to see it. It is about balance: not being affected by what you cannot control, while still letting yourself feel your emotions. Without that balance there is a risk of becoming unempathetic and detached from your own feelings.

The biggest benefit for me is control over my emotions. It teaches you to stay unaffected by external events, so you can make more rational decisions and act with clarity whatever the circumstances.

Stoicism also says we cannot control fate. I’m still coming to terms with that. I believe we can shape our lives and make choices, and I’m starting to understand the Stoic view: accept what you cannot control and make the best possible outcome from it.

Nothing external to you can truly harm you. Externals cannot harm your moral character or diminish your wellbeing. Only your thoughts can bring about that harm, so don’t allow it.

Our judgements of others do not harm them, they only harm us. Pay attention to your judgement of external events, challenge them, release them.

You don’t need a philosophy

You don’t have to follow a specific philosophy to improve yourself. My own journey is just getting started, and I don’t have all the answers. Find something that helps you grow, makes you mentally stronger and brings you peace of mind. Don’t force a mindset on yourself because someone says you should. Stoicism helped me, but that doesn’t mean it will work for you. Let what fits you evolve slowly over time.

Recommended reading

Berserk, or Vagabond, which I’m reading now. They may only be manga or graphic novels, but they can change your life if you let them. The lessons in these stories run deep and can inspire growth in ways you wouldn’t expect.

Habits

Discipline and consistency

These are two of the most important qualities in life, in the gym and out of it. Stay consistent with your food, training, sleep, work and relationships, and each of them will improve far faster than it would without consistency.

Discipline

Do it anyway

The art of doing what needs to be done, whether you feel like it or not.

Waking up early to train even when you’re tired.

Consistency

Do it again

Doing the right things over and over, day after day.

Doing that every single day until it becomes a routine.

Where I started

When I was younger I was a gaming addict who barely went outside. At 18 I knew something had to change. I wanted my life to mean something, and I didn’t want to waste my time, because time is the most valuable resource we have. I started like most people, at home, with a scuffed, rusty bench. Not ideal, but real.

My first workouts were fueled by a deep desire to change. After a month or two I saw progress. My body began to transform, slowly but surely. From there the gym and the power of delayed gratification became a positive feedback loop that fed my hunger for more.

The first phase

The hardest part is getting through the period before you see results. For some people it takes longer than for others, but everyone can change. Don’t compare your journey to anyone else’s. Everyone is running their own race, and comparison is the thief of joy.

At the beginning, motivation helps you survive the days when quitting feels easier. It can carry you through that critical early phase until habits form.

The hard days

Even after six months, one year or two years, there are still hard days. How you act when you’re tired, unmotivated and frustrated is what will either break you or make you.

What pushes me harder is thinking back to the little boy who just wanted to be liked, and imagining how proud he would be to see me now, doing the things we once only dreamed of. When I feel down or weak, I picture my older self, disappointed and sad that I gave up when things got tough. That thought is enough to push me through almost anything.

Don’t follow me blindly

I got extreme with self-improvement at times, and I developed some unhealthy habits along the way. You don’t have to do it like I did. Take things at your own pace and find what works for you. I like pushing myself to the limit, but you don’t have to.

Set goals that mean something

Make your goals realistic and meaningful. Don’t aim for something so unreachable that it crushes your motivation. A common goal in the gym is a 100 kg bench press, and if that works for you, great. Maybe you’re a father who wants to be fit enough to play more with your kids. Any goal is valid as long as it has meaning to you.

The only real failure is giving up on yourself.

Character

Ego and humility

Keeps you grounded

Gratitude

Being thankful for what you have been given, whether or not you are religious.

Keeps you real

Humility

The attitude that you have no special importance that makes you better than others. You recognize your own value without believing it puts you above anyone else.

Keeps you moving forward

Pride

Acknowledging your own progress and being content with it. Bettering yourself naturally builds pride, and you should be proud of your hard work and growth.

Pulls you backward

Ego

An inflated feeling of pride in your superiority over others. The belief that you are better, more important or more deserving than those around you.

Don’t let pride turn into ego. Stay humble, because you don’t know someone else’s journey, and you can’t judge where someone is in life just by looking at them.

You will meet annoying, difficult people. Often they act that way because of insecurity, pain or a lack of competence, and they compensate through their behavior. Understanding this doesn’t mean you have to accept bad behavior. It reminds you not to fall into arrogance yourself.

Humility doesn’t mean believing you have no value. Your presence, actions and efforts have meaning, just like everyone else’s. Find the balance between staying grounded and being proud of what you’ve accomplished. Respect yourself and the work you’ve put in, and never put others down to validate yourself. Real strength is being proud without being arrogant, and being humble without forgetting your worth.

Practice gratitude

Be grateful for everything: waking up in the morning, being able to read this, being able to walk, breathe and go to the gym to work on yourself.

Gratitude shows you how many unnecessary problems we create for ourselves. Many people around the world would dream of having the life you have right now. Never take it for granted.

Gratitude keeps you grounded. Humility keeps you real. Pride keeps you moving forward. But ego will always pull you backward. Choose wisely.

Long game

Continuous improvement

Don’t rush the process, and don’t think it ever truly ends. Improvement isn’t about doing all the right things at once. It is about gradually building a healthy mindset that brings peace and happiness. Over time the way you think and view the world shifts, shaped by new experiences and unexpected events.

Progress isn’t always loud or visible. Sometimes it is in the quiet choices you make daily. Get just 1% better each day, a 0.01 gain, and that small gain compounds. Over a year, 1 becomes 37.78. Drag the slider to see it build.

So don’t underestimate the little things. They aren’t just steps on the path. They are the path.

Fundamentals

The three macronutrients

I split fitness into three parts: training at 40%, diet at 40% and recovery at 20%. Nutrition breaks down into protein, carbohydrates and fats. The exact ratio depends on your goal, whether that is bulking, cutting or maintenance, but these guidelines are a solid starting point.

The building block

Protein

1.8–2.2g per kg bodyweight

Essential for muscle growth and repair. I weigh 95 kg, so I aim for around 200 g a day.

  • Chicken breast
  • Lean ground beef
  • Protein shakes
  • Eggs and egg whites
  • Low-fat dairy
The main fuel

Carbohydrates

4–5g per kg, my current intake

Your body’s preferred fuel, especially in intense training. Stored as glycogen in muscle and liver, carbs affect your performance and how your muscles look, full or flat. At 95 kg I eat 380–475 g a day, roughly 1,520–1,900 kcal.

  • White rice
  • Pasta
  • Potatoes
  • Fruits
  • Vegetables
  • Some bread, in moderation
Crucial for hormones

Fats

Don’t go lowno fixed target

Many people minimize fat, especially when cutting, but going too low backfires. Too little can lower testosterone, increase the risk of acne and sometimes lead to gynecomastia. I made this mistake: when I cut fats too aggressively my hormones got out of balance and I had noticeable side effects.

  • Whole eggs
  • Fatty fish: salmon, mackerel
  • Nuts and seeds
  • Olive oil
  • Avocados

Energy balance

Bulking and cutting

Changing your body comes down to one principle: calories in versus calories out. First find your maintenance calories, the amount that keeps your weight stable. It depends on your basal metabolic rate (BMR), what your body burns at rest, and your daily activity, from workouts to walking around.

Use an online calculator or track your weight over a few weeks to get a solid number, then make small adjustments for your goal.

Gain muscle

Bulking

Eat slightly above maintenance. Bulking is a bit easier than cutting because you have more room to fit your macros.

  • Start at +250 to +500 kcal a day.
  • Don’t overshoot. Too many extra calories just become fat.
  • Keep protein steady at 1.8–2.2 g per kg.
  • Add the extra calories as carbs and fats.
  • Monitor your weight and adjust weekly.
Lose fat

Cutting

Create a slight deficit by eating fewer calories than you burn.

  • Raise protein to 2.2–2.5 g per kg to preserve muscle.
  • Reduce carbs and fats gradually. Don’t crash your intake.
  • Stay active and keep strength training.
  • Make small adjustments weekly based on how your body responds.
Conditioning

Cardio

Don’t skip cardio. It’s easy to see it only as a fat-loss tool, but its benefits go further. Cardio improves your cardiovascular health, supports recovery, builds endurance and can even extend your lifespan. It is about your overall quality of life, not just getting lean.

Even during a bulk, stay active. A walk, a swim or a bike ride won’t sabotage your muscle gains. It can support them by improving blood flow and recovery.

When you cut, cardio that is already part of your routine makes the fat loss phase easier, because the extra calories burned add up over the week.

Recovery

More is not always better. Progress doesn’t come from constant grinding. It comes from proper recovery, and quality beats quantity every time.

Muscle recovery

Quality beats quantity

When I first started, I made the mistake of training every single day, thinking more sessions would mean faster results. Instead I saw slow progress and constant fatigue.

Recovery is personal. Some people bounce back quickly, while others, like me, need more time, especially after an injury. Since my shoulder injury I’ve learned to be extra cautious and to build my training split around it, so I avoid overworking the joint and stay injury-free.

Rest days

Don’t skip them

Rest days let your muscles repair and grow.

Sleep
8 h+per night, at least

Sleep doesn’t just affect recovery. It also plays a crucial role in testosterone production and overall hormone balance.

Soreness

Listen to your body

If a muscle is still sore, it hasn’t fully recovered. Training it again too soon stops you from training at full intensity and can slow your progress.

Training smart includes knowing when to back off. That’s how real, long-term gains are made.

My position

PEDs

Don’t do them.

I don’t do them, and you don’t need them, especially in your first couple of years of training. Building muscle takes time, effort and consistency. PEDs might speed up the process, but at a huge cost. You’ll see faster growth, but true progress isn’t just the size of your muscles. It is strength, fortitude and mental health.

Serious risks include
  • Hormonal imbalances
  • Liver damage
  • Increased risk of heart disease
  • Mental health issues

You’re starting on the natural path, and that’s all you need. There’s no magic pill or shortcut. The real gains come from hard work, patience and dedication over time. I don’t hate people who openly use PEDs, but for 99% of people they’re simply not needed. You won’t find any advice on PEDs here.

Before every session

Warm up first

Warming up and stretching is non-negotiable.

Every session I start with elastic bands, dynamic stretches and ideally 5 minutes of walking on the treadmill to get my body warm. It isn’t optional. It helps prevent injuries and gets your body ready to train.

Splits

Two ways to run push, pull, legs

For beginners I recommend a classic push, pull, legs routine. It’s simple, effective and easy to recover from when intensity stays in check. You hit each muscle group twice a week, which suits beginners as long as recovery is managed.

Push, rest, pull, legs, rest, then repeat. Because the cycle is 5 days long it ignores the calendar week, so you train 4 to 5 days a week depending on where the cycle falls. Once every 5 weeks you get 5 training days in one week.

It prioritizes recovery, which matters with a past injury like a shoulder. It works well if your schedule is flexible and isn’t ideal for a fixed weekly routine.

Why this works
  • Every push day is followed by a rest day, so your shoulders and pressing muscles get more time to recover.
  • You never train push and pull back to back, so your upper body isn’t overworked.
  • You can push harder in each session because you’re not running on fatigue.
  • With a lingering injury you can recover without skipping whole weeks.
Intensity

Every set to failure

In this split you don’t manage reps in reserve (RIR). You take every set to failure, pushing each exercise to its limit, and aim for growth through full exhaustion.

The starter program is the opposite: control and form first, no absolute failure.

Progress

Progressive overload

If you want serious muscle and strength, progressive overload isn’t optional. It is the foundation. It means gradually increasing the weight, reps or intensity of an exercise over time. Without it you’re repeating workouts, not improving, and you’ll hit plateaus fast because your body adapts to what you give it.

The easiest and most effective way is to track your sets and reps every session. Once you hit a personal threshold, you increase the weight. Mine: on most exercises I add weight after hitting 10+ reps. On heavy lifts like squats I add weight once I go above 7 reps.

Track with a notebook, which is old school but still effective, or an app that logs sets and weights, like Arrow. The key is consistency. Track everything.

Type of lift

If you plateau

First make sure it’s a real plateau, not just a bad day or week. If it is, here is how I break it.

Option one

Change the rep range

Drop to lower reps (3–6) and go heavier to build strength. Once you’re stronger, return to your usual range and smash it.

Option two

Slow down your tempo

Focus on the eccentric, the lowering phase, to really tax your muscles. More time under tension means more growth.

Option three

Swap the exercise

Replace it with a similar movement for a few weeks. When you come back, you’ll usually break through the sticking point.

Option four

Just eat more

Simple but true. If all else fails, more food means more fuel and more strength. See the Nutrition chapter.
